About

From the tropics to the Arctic

One question runs through my work: what happens to a sea when it warms up and we keep fishing it? To explore it, I build food-web models, digital versions of an ecosystem in which every group, from microorganisms to top predators and fishing fleets, eats and is eaten. Then I run them into the future. My main tool is Ecopath with Ecosim and Ecospace (EwE).

My academic path has followed the sea. It began in Brazil, where I modelled the deep shelf and slope off the southeastern Brazilian coast. Then I moved to the Arctic, where I modelled the Barents Sea during The Nansen Legacy project at UiT The Arctic University of Norway. Since 2023, I have lived in Germany, where I am part of the Ecosystem Modelling group at GEOMAR in Kiel. There I study the Western Baltic Sea: how multiple stressors shape the ecosystem, and how fisheries management can help it cope with climate change.

Research is not my only background. For many years I did environmental fieldwork and assessments in Brazil, in the Atlantic Forest, the Amazon and the Cerrado. I also taught ecology and environmental policy at university and helped build the Brazilian Air Force's environmental management system. That practical side still shapes how I work: I want my models to be useful to the people who make decisions about the sea.

Research

Ecosystem models

Each model is a map of who eats whom, from algae to top predators and fishing boats. Ecospace turns it into a real map: animals move across a grid following temperature, food, predators and fishing, so we can see where they may live in the future.

2023 – present · GEOMAR · SpaCeParti · EcoScope

Western Baltic Sea

The Western Baltic Sea Ecospace model is adapted from the Ecopath model of Scotti et al. (2022). This spatial version asks how warming, different levels of primary production, oxygen and salinity, and different fishing rules, including marine protected areas, could change where fish live and what fishers catch until 2100. The same model also runs inside the Western Baltic edition of the MSP Challenge game.

MSP Challenge · Western Baltic Sea

MSP Challenge is a game for planning the sea. Planners and stakeholders decide where wind farms, shipping lanes, cables and protected areas go, and watch how the ecosystem responds. I developed the ecosystem model behind the Western Baltic edition.

Along the way I split the old, general "noise" pressure into impulsive noise, continuous noise and bottom vibration, added disturbance from the air, and created a way to score how strongly each pressure affects each species group. I joined a session with European stakeholders in Brussels as a specialist, and led one for the SpaCeParti team.

Barents Sea

The Barents Sea is where Atlantic and Arctic waters meet, and with them boreal and Arctic communities, divided mostly by the Polar Front. I built the spatial Ecospace model on the Ecopath with Ecosim model of Pedersen et al. (2021). Checked against survey data, the Ecospace model reproduces what scientists have seen in warm years: species moving poleward (Nascimento et al. 2023). It then looks ahead, combining low- and high-emission climate scenarios with different fishing strategies (Nascimento et al. 2025).

Southern Brazilian shelf and slope

My first model, from my PhD, describes who eats whom among the fish and invertebrates living near the bottom on the outer shelf and upper slope of southeastern Brazil. It is based on stomach contents collected by the REVIZEE programme. Later I used it to ask how changes in plankton production could affect fish such as the blackfin goosefish.
